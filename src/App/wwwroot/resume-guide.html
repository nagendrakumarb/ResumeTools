How to use ATS and job-match results responsibly

ATS compatibility and job matching are different

An ATS compatibility scan asks whether the document has readable text and recognizable resume structure. It can inspect headings, contact details, dates, word count, action language, measurable results, and formatting signals without a job description.

A job-match review asks whether the resume contains truthful evidence related to one selected role. A structurally excellent resume can still have a low match for an unrelated job. A strong keyword match can still be a poor resume if the document is difficult to parse.

How to read the percentage

The percentage is a summary of this application's checks. Review the individual metrics and evidence before the total. A lower score identifies areas to investigate; it does not prove rejection. A high score does not represent approval by an employer.

Start with a readable source document

A useful review begins with the resume you would actually send, not a simplified copy made only for a scanner. Export a fresh PDF or DOCX file, open it on a second device when possible, and make sure the text can be selected. A scanned image of a resume may look correct while providing little usable text to an applicant tracking system.

Use ordinary section names such as Experience, Education, Skills, Projects, and Certifications when they describe the content. Decorative headings and text placed inside images can make a document harder to interpret. Tables, columns, icons, and colour are not automatically wrong, but they should not be the only way a reader can discover dates, employer names, job titles, or key achievements.

A practical way to interpret an ATS check

  1. Read the observation behind a metric before changing anything.
  2. Fix factual or formatting problems first, such as an unreadable file, missing contact detail, or an unclear section heading.
  3. Keep a copy of the original resume so each change can be compared with the version you submitted.
  4. Read the final document from top to bottom. A higher tool score is not useful if the resume is less accurate or less clear to a person.

For example, a report may note few measurable achievements. The right response is not to invent numbers. If you can support a result with records or a clear estimate, describe the scope honestly: “reduced weekly reconciliation time by about two hours” is more useful than a vague claim of “improved efficiency.” If you cannot verify a number, explain the responsibility without one.

Do not add every missing word

Job descriptions contain benefits, legal statements, company descriptions, education alternatives, and ordinary connecting words. These are not automatically resume skills. Add a phrase only when it is relevant, meaningful, and supported by your real work.

Good example: a job requires “REST API design,” and the resume already describes building web services. The phrase may be clarified in that experience bullet. Bad example: adding “bachelor's,” “typically,” or “minimum” to Skills merely because those words occurred in the posting.

Place evidence in the right section

Technologies can appear in Skills, but evidence is stronger in a relevant achievement. Communication or leadership should normally be demonstrated by an experience bullet rather than listed without context. Education requirements belong in Education only when the applicant actually holds them.

Tailor one role at a time

Start by identifying the role's central work: the outcomes it owns, the systems or methods it uses, and the level of responsibility. Then compare those points with experience you can prove. This approach produces a focused resume without turning every application into a completely different biography.

Suppose a posting asks for SQL reporting, stakeholder communication, and process improvement. If you have created reporting dashboards and presented findings to an operations team, make those examples easy to find. Do not claim database administration, a management title, or a particular industry background unless they are true. A recruiter should be able to ask about every tailored phrase and receive a straightforward answer.

Write achievements that a person can understand

A strong experience bullet usually answers three questions: what did you do, in what context, and what changed? Begin with a precise action, name the relevant process or tool, and then state a truthful outcome. The outcome can be a number, a delivered feature, a reduced source of error, a completed handover, or a decision that your work supported.

Instead of “Responsible for reports,” try “Prepared weekly inventory reports in Excel for the warehouse lead, highlighting exceptions for follow-up.” If the work led to a verified result, add it; if it did not, clarity is still better than an inflated metric. Keep bullet points specific enough to show contribution but short enough to scan.

Preserve facts and formatting

Review generated DOCX files. Confirm that headings remain recognizable, employers and roles retain emphasis, dates remain attached to the correct positions, and no Projects, Education, or Experience section disappeared. Page reduction should begin with excessive spacing and repetition, not automatic deletion of whole sections.

Verify job listings

Open the original application page and confirm the employer, location, posting date, requirements, and application method. Never pay a recruiter for a job, disclose unnecessary identity documents, or treat a warning score as proof that a company is fraudulent.

Check the final version before applying

Open the generated file in the program you plan to use. Check page breaks, alignment, links, dates, and bullet indentation. Confirm that your name and contact information are current, that the target role is correct, and that each company, qualification, and achievement remains yours. When an application system requests a PDF, export the reviewed DOCX rather than relying on a preview alone.

Keep a simple record of the version sent for each application. It helps you answer follow-up questions accurately and prevents accidentally reusing a tailored statement for an unrelated employer. The goal is a clear, truthful document that makes review easier for both software and people.

Suggested workflow

  1. Upload the original resume and review ATS compatibility.
  2. Correct only observations that are accurate.
  3. Select one real job and import its full description.
  4. Review strong matches and meaningful gaps.
  5. Add only evidence-backed improvements.
  6. Download and inspect the final document in Word.
  7. Recalculate, verify the original application URL, and apply.
